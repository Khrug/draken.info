## §0 Sammanfattning

*Den som smyger stjäl avståndet, inte bytet.*

To stalk is to shrink a neighbourhood around a target while keeping one's own estimates of it consistent and one's own presence out of its view; the sheaf-theoretic stalk, the set of germs at a point, is what survives that shrinking **[D]**. The post reads one sound three ways: the covert approach of predators and intelligence services, the eye raised on a stalk in snails and flies, and the stalk of a sheaf, with the Dungeons & Dragons Beholder and the Strugatsky–Tarkovsky Stalker as two cultural limit cases **[D]**. The wordplay that opens it (stal king, wall king, pre dator) is tagged root by root: two of the cuts touch real etymology, the rest are pointers only **[E/M]**. The post does not claim that cats, monitors, stalkers and intelligence services compute colimits, nor that the coincidence of the plant word and the hunting word in English explains either **[D]**.

## §1 Three words, cut open

### §1.1 Stalk: two roots in one sound [E]

English *stalk* is two words. The noun, the stem of a plant, is a diminutive of Middle English *stale* (upright of a ladder, handle), from the PIE root *\*stel-* "to put, stand, put in order", the root of things that stand in a place (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. The verb, to approach stealthily, comes from Old English *-stealcian* in *bestealcian* "to steal along, walk warily"; its Germanic form is a frequentative, and the dictionary connects it, with a "possibly", to the root behind *steal* and *stealth* (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[S]**. A second verb *stalk*, "to walk haughtily with stiff high steps", appears in the 1520s and means nearly the opposite of creeping (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. *Stalker* is attested as "one who stalks game" from the early fifteenth century; "harass obsessively" dates from about 1991 (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**.

So one English sound holds the standing stem, the creeping thief and the stately strut. The mathematical *stalk* of §2 borrows the plant noun, and the predation of §3 is the creeping verb **[E]**. That they share a spelling is history; that they share a structure is the claim of this post, and it rests on §2 and §3, not on the spelling **[D]**.

### §1.2 Stal king: the thief and the stable [M]

Cut *stalk-ing* after the *l*. In Swedish *stal* is the past tense of *stjäla*, to steal, which makes *stal king* "the king who stole" **[M]**. Because the English hunting verb may descend from the steal-root, this cut lands closer to a real root than most paronomasia does; the link is "possibly" in the dictionary, so it carries no weight here (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[H]**. The other half of the sound, the standing root *\*stel-*, is the one behind *stall* and Swedish *ställa*: the place where something is set **[M]**. The stal-king is therefore both the one who steals across a boundary and the one who keeps a stall, a fixed place **[M]**. The Swedish plant word *stjälk* sounds like *stjäla* with a *k*, which is a pointer, not a derivation **[M]**.

### §1.3 Wall king: the walk that fulls cloth [E/M]

*Walk* comes from Old English *wealcan* "to toss, roll" and *wealcian* "to roll up", from a Germanic root whose cousins mean to knead or full cloth, Old Norse *valka*, Danish *valke*, German *walken* (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. Swedish *valka*, to full, belongs to the same family **[S]**. *Wall*, by contrast, is the Latin *vallum*, the palisade, and has nothing to do with walking **[E]**. So *wall king* is pure sound **[M]**. It still names something the corpus uses: the open walker on the wall is the visible keeper of a boundary, the patrol whose job is to be seen ([The Keeper at the Crossing](/posts/the-keeper-at-the-crossing/), DRK-183) **[D]**. Walking is the gait that wants to be seen; stalking (verb 1) is the gait that does not; the 1520s stalk (verb 2) is the king's gait, which wants to be seen and feared **[D]**.

### §1.4 Pre dator: the one who computes ahead [M]

*Predator* is Latin *praedator* "plunderer", from *praedari* "to rob", from *praeda* "booty, prey", itself from *prae-heda* "something seized before", related to *prehendere* "to grasp" (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. The *-dator* is the Latin agent ending on *praeda*, not the Latin *dator* "giver" **[E]**. Cut it anyway: Swedish *dator* is the computer, so *pre-dator* reads "the one before the computer", or "the computer that runs ahead" **[M]**. The second reading points at a real result: a dragonfly intercepting prey steers with internal models that predict both the prey's motion and its own body's response, rather than simply chasing where the prey is (Mischiati et al. 2015) **[S]**. Interception requires prediction **[S]**. The pun is a pointer; the forward model is the referent, and the argument below would stand without the pun **[D]**.

## §2 Formal sketch: the stalk as limit of a pursuit (analogy, not derivation)

Let $X$ be a topological space and $\mathcal F$ a sheaf of sets on $X$: to each open set $U \subseteq X$ it assigns a set $\mathcal F(U)$ of sections, and to each inclusion $V \subseteq U$ a restriction map $\rho_{UV}: \mathcal F(U) \to \mathcal F(V)$, with the gluing axioms. The **stalk** of $\mathcal F$ at a point $x \in X$ is the direct limit

$$\mathcal F_x \;=\; \varinjlim_{U \ni x} \mathcal F(U),$$

taken over all open neighbourhoods of $x$. Its elements are **germs**: two sections $s \in \mathcal F(U)$, $t \in \mathcal F(V)$ define the same germ $[s]_x = [t]_x$ when they agree on some smaller open $W \subseteq U \cap V$ containing $x$ (Hartshorne 1977) **[E]**.

Now take a nested sequence of neighbourhoods $U_0 \supseteq U_1 \supseteq U_2 \supseteq \cdots$ of $x$ and sections $s_n \in \mathcal F(U_n)$ with $s_{n+1} = \rho_{U_n U_{n+1}}(s_n)$ for all $n$. Then every $s_n$ defines one and the same germ $[s_0]_x$, directly from the definition **[E]**. If the $U_n$ form a neighbourhood basis at $x$, the germ is all that the sequence determines at the point **[E]**.

The reading. Let $X$ be the stalker's space of possible target states (position, heading, intent), and $\mathcal F(U)$ the set of hypotheses about the target that are consistent with everything observed over $U$ **[D]**. A **stalk** in the hunting sense is then a pursuit that shrinks the neighbourhood $U_n$ around the target while keeping each new estimate a restriction of the old one **[D]**. Losing track is a step where $s_{n+1} \neq \rho(s_n)$: the new estimate contradicts the old, and the pursuit has to widen $U$ again **[D]**. The pounce, or in the corpus's own vocabulary the bite of [The Bite](/posts/the-bite/) (DRK-184), is the commitment to the germ: acting on what holds in every arbitrarily small neighbourhood of the target, rather than on what holds over a wide region **[D]**.

**Stalking** (proposed corpus term): a pursuit that refines a nested family of neighbourhoods around a target while keeping its own estimates restriction-compatible and keeping its own contribution to the target's observations at zero **[D]**. The first two clauses are the sheaf stalk; the third is §4.

Detection is the dual problem. The prey also holds a sheaf of observations over its own sensory field, glance by glance **[D]**. In the cellular-sheaf formalism, disagreement between local sections on their overlaps is measured by the sheaf Laplacian, and zero disagreement is exactly a global section (Hansen & Ghrist 2019) **[E]**. The prey that notices "the bush moved between glances" has found two local sections that fail to agree on their overlap; in the corpus's $H^1$ language, the stalker becomes visible as an obstruction **[D]**. The [Stalking Cell](/posts/the-stalking-cell/) (DRK-167) used the same word for a cell pursuing a morphogenetic setpoint it cannot see; this post supplies the topology that the earlier pun assumed **[D]**.

## §3 Four stalkers

### §3.1 The cat: the visual stalk

Leyhausen's classic studies of domestic and wild cats describe predation as a sequence: locating, a low crouched approach that alternates runs with motionless pauses, a final lurk, and the pounce (Leyhausen 1979) **[S]**. Across terrestrial species, vertical slit pupils are associated with ambush predators that are active by day and night and have their eyes close to the ground, and the slit is argued to sharpen the depth cues needed to judge the distance of the pounce (Banks et al. 2015) **[S]**. The cat is a point-stalker: its neighbourhood shrinks in space, around a target it can see, and its last act is a distance estimate **[D]**.

### §3.2 The water monitor: the chemical trail

Monitor lizards are dietary generalists by the standards of lizards, and large species such as *Varanus salvator* take a wide range of vertebrate and invertebrate prey and carrion (Losos & Greene 1988) **[S]**. In the foraging-mode framework of Huey & Pianka (1981), the contrast is between sit-and-wait predators and wide-ranging active foragers; varanids sit at the active end **[S]**. Squamates with deeply forked tongues, varanids and snakes, can sample chemicals at two points at once, and the forked tongue is argued to work as an edge detector for following a chemical trail (Schwenk 1994) **[S]**.

So the monitor stalks a different object from the cat **[D]**. The cat narrows a neighbourhood around a point; the monitor follows a section along a path, a trail laid down by the prey's earlier passage, and the two tongue tips test whether the section continues left or right **[D]**. The cat's question is *where exactly*; the monitor's is *which way does it continue* **[D]**. These are two regimes of the same operation: restriction to a shrinking region versus continuation along a curve **[D]**.

### §3.3 Human predators: tracker and stalker

In the persistence-hunting model of human evolution, two factors are named as critical: cooling by sweat while running in the heat, and the ability to track an animal down (Liebenberg 2006) **[S]**. Human hunting, in that model, is trail-reading plus endurance: the monitor's regime raised to inference over hours, with traces read as evidence of where the animal went and in what state **[D]**.

The same verb now names a crime. In a forensic-psychiatric study of 145 stalkers, Mullen and colleagues proposed a typology of five types, including a *predatory* type whose surveillance of the victim prepares a sexual assault (Mullen et al. 1999) **[S]**. Structurally, the predatory stalker runs the hunting sequence of §2 against a person: covert collection, a shrinking neighbourhood around the victim's routine, an act prepared at the germ **[D]**. The structural match describes; it neither naturalises nor excuses, and the victim is not prey in any sense that carries moral weight **[D]**. What the form does explain is why the harm begins long before any act: the victim's private neighbourhood is being taken away one restriction at a time **[D]**.

### §3.4 Military intelligence: the institutional stalk

US joint doctrine divides intelligence operations into six categories: planning and direction; collection; processing and exploitation; analysis and production; dissemination and integration; evaluation and feedback (Joint Chiefs of Staff 2013) **[E]**. Read against §2, planning and direction picks the point $x$; collection gathers local sections over many overlapping $U$; processing and analysis glue them into a consistent picture; dissemination hands the germ to whoever acts on it **[D]**. Evaluation and feedback is the step where $s_{n+1} \neq \rho(s_n)$ is noticed and the neighbourhood widened again **[D]**.

The hunting lexicon already contains the deception step. A *stalking-horse* (1510s) was a horse a fowler hid behind to get within range of game; by the 1610s the word meant a person or proceeding used to disguise a real purpose (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. Deception operations are stalking-horses at institutional scale **[D]**. An intelligence service is a wall king and a stal king at once: counter-intelligence walks its own wall in the open, and collection steals across someone else's **[M]**.

## §4 Stealth as a vanishing section

The third clause of the definition in §2, keeping one's own contribution to the target's observations at zero, has an exact optical version **[D]**. Srinivasan & Davey (1995) showed that a pursuer can camouflage its motion by moving so that it stays on the line between the target and a fixed reference point; the target then sees the pursuer as stationary, with no motion across its retina beyond looming **[S]**. Mizutani et al. (2003) reported that territorial dragonflies use this strategy against each other **[S]**. The cat's freeze when the prey looks up does the same job with less geometry: no change between glances, so nothing to disagree on an overlap (Leyhausen 1979) **[S]**.

In the language of §2, the stalker arranges that its image is a locally constant section of the prey's observation sheaf, so successive glances glue without obstruction **[D]**. Stealth is not invisibility; it is consistency **[D]**. The stalker is seen and is not detected, because what is seen agrees with what was seen a moment ago **[D]**. The stalking-horse of §3.4 is the same trick with a decoy: a section the target already accepts is offered as cover for one it would reject **[D]**.

## §5 The eye on the stalk

Many gastropods carry eyes on tentacles or short stalks, and in land snails the eyes sit at the tips of retractable upper tentacles (Chase 2002) **[S]**. The apple snail *Pomacea canaliculata* has a camera-type eye on a short eyestalk, regenerates the complete eye after resection, and needs the gene *pax6* to form it, which makes it a genetic model for whole-eye regeneration (Accorsi et al. 2025) **[S]**. In stalk-eyed flies (Diopsidae), the span between the males' eyes is an exaggerated sexual trait; when the span was changed by artificial selection over generations, female preference shifted with it (Wilkinson & Reillo 1994) **[S]**.

An eyestalk moves the point of sampling **[D]**. The organism puts its sensor at the end of a stalk to change which neighbourhood its section is taken over: higher, wider, further from its own body **[D]**. In the fly, the stalk length becomes a signal in its own right, so the instrument of looking is also something to be looked at **[D]**. In the regenerating snail the stalk keeps the address where the eye must regrow, which is the [Stalking Cell](/posts/the-stalking-cell/) (DRK-167) result at organ scale: a structure pursuing a setpoint it does not contain **[D]**. The eye on a stalk and the stalk of a sheaf share one image: the point where a whole is read locally **[M]**.

## §6 The Beholder: a sphere of stalks

The Beholder entered *Dungeons & Dragons* in the first rules supplement, *Greyhawk*, as a floating sphere with a large central eye, a mouth and ten small eyestalks, each eye carrying a different magical power (Gygax & Kuntz 1975) **[E]**. In later editions the central eye projects a cone of antimagic in which magical effects fail, the creature's own eye rays included (Wizards of the Coast 2014) **[S]**. *Behold* is Old English *bihaldan* "to hold in view, keep", a West Germanic compound shared with German *behalten* and Swedish *behålla*; the application to watching is confined to English (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. The Beholder is, by its English name, a keeper, the role analysed as keeper-function in [The Generalizard](/posts/the-generalizard/) (DRK-150) **[M]**.

As a sheaf diagram the monster is exact enough to be useful **[D]**. Ten stalks each carry a local section with its own effect; the central eye carries the zero section over its cone, the section that cancels every other section it falls on **[D]**. The rules make it unable to glue: wherever the central gaze points, its own stalk powers vanish, so to use its stalks it must look away from its target **[D]**. The total gaze cancels itself. It is the [Totalitarian Sheaf](/posts/the-totalitarian-sheaf/) (DRK-125) drawn as a monster: the observer that wants every point in one look loses the local powers that made looking worth anything **[D]**. [Övervakaren och Undervakaren](/posts/overvakaren-och-undervakaren/) (DRK-178) argued that the watch, not the eye, is what was inherited; the Beholder is the eye with no watch, only seeing and cancelling **[D]**.

This is also the anti-totalisation principle applied to Draken itself **[D]**. A framework that tried to hold every layer in its central eye would erase the local sections it was built to glue **[D]**.

## §7 Stalker: the zone that stalks back

The Russian word *сталкер* was coined by Arkady and Boris Strugatsky for *Roadside Picnic* (1972), where it names illegal prospectors who bring alien artefacts out of the Zone; Boris Strugatsky later explained it as an allusion to Kipling's *Stalky* (Strugatsky 2003) **[S]**. In Kipling's school stories, Stalky's chief adversary among the masters is the housemaster King (Kipling 1899) **[E]**. Stalky, who outwits King, is the stal-king in the novel that gave Russian its word **[M]**.

In Tarkovsky's *Stalker* (1979), the Stalker guides a Writer and a Professor through the Zone to a Room said to grant one's innermost wish, testing each stretch of the path by throwing metal nuts tied with strips of cloth (Tarkovsky 1979) **[E]**. The *S.T.A.L.K.E.R.* games carry the Zone to the Chernobyl exclusion area and keep the probe: the player throws bolts to reveal anomalies before stepping (GSC Game World 2007) **[S]**.

The Stalker inverts every case in §3 **[D]**. He is not the predator; the Zone is, and he survives by stalking the terrain itself, one sampled neighbourhood at a time **[D]**. Each thrown nut is a test section over a small open set, taken before the body commits; the straight path is never safe because the local rules do not glue into a global map **[D]**. The Room is the stalk at the point: in the film's logic it grants the innermost wish, not the wish one states, so it reads the germ and not the section one would present over a wider neighbourhood **[D]**. That is the reason the Stalker fears it **[D]**.

## §8 Falsification (DRK-131)

**F1: Narrowing.** §2 and §3.1 claim that a stalk refines a neighbourhood around the target and widens it again after loss of track. **Refuted** if gaze or head-tracking data from stalking felids or intercepting predators show no progressive narrowing of the sampled region during approach, or show wide and narrow sampling alternating with no relation to moments when the target was lost.

**F2: Stealth as consistency.** §4 claims that detection follows mismatch between successive observations more than nearness. **Refuted** if, with looming and distance matched, motion-camouflaged approaches and uncamouflaged approaches are detected by prey at equal rates.

**F3: Two regimes.** §3.2 claims that the water monitor's approach is trail-continuation, driven by chemical sampling, rather than visual point-narrowing. **Refuted** if tongue-flick rate in *Varanus salvator* during approach to prey is unaffected by the presence or absence of a prey chemical trail, with visual cues held constant.

**F4: Scope limit.** The etymological cuts in §1 are illustrations; except where tagged [E], none of them supports the argument, and the post survives their removal. The sheaf construction in §2 is exact mathematics; its application to animals, people and institutions is a structural reading, not a claim that any of them computes a direct limit. The readings of the Beholder and the Stalker in §6–§7 interpret fictions and test nothing. If F1–F3 fail, §2 remains correct as mathematics and §5–§7 remain as readings, but the claim that stalking *is* stalk-computation is withdrawn.
