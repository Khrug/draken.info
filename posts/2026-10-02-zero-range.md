## §0 Sammanfattning

*Människan saknade klor, så hon lärde sig kasta dem.*

*Range* is, at its root, a ring around a point. Free range, weapon range and zero range are three radii of one circle, and the last of them is the point itself, the place where two bodies stop being separate and become a *mêlée*, a mixture **[E/D]**. Nearly every animal that kills does so at or near that point, with teeth, claws, venom and armour; the few animals that throw, spit or shoot reach centimetres or a few metres, and mostly to stun, blind or deter **[S]**. The human is the exception. Poorly armed at zero range, with small canines, flat nails and bare skin, our lineage became the only animal that kills reliably from tens of metres, first by throwing and then with artefacts that throw for it **[S]**. This post follows that exception from the words that carry it, through the animals that never needed it, to the bow, the artillery shell and the drone, and argues that distance is not only a tactical variable but a moral one: the further away the target, the easier the gate opens **[D]**. It does not claim that range explains human violence; it claims that range is one of its axes, and an unusually clean one to measure **[D]**.

## §1 The ring

Start with the word. *Range* entered English around 1200 as *renge*, a row or line of persons, especially of hunters or soldiers, from Old French *reng* "row, rank", which came from a Frankish word for a ring or circle, from the PIE root *\*sker-* "to turn, bend" (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. Its later senses unfold from that row of hunters like rings in water: a line of mountains by 1705, the scope or extent of something by the late fifteenth century, and from the 1620s the area over which animals roam in search of food (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**.

So the oldest picture inside the word is a line of armed men, and behind it a circle **[E]**. That is exactly what a battue looks like: hunters drawn up in a curved line, closing on the animals inside it **[D]**. The ring is the shape of a hunt before it is the shape of anything else in the word **[D]**.

Read the three ranges against that circle **[D]**. *Free range* is the widest ring, the open grazing land where the animal moves without a fence; its radius is the animal's own wandering **[D]**. *Weapon range* is the ring drawn around a shooter: the distance inside which a stone, an arrow or a bullet still arrives with enough force to wound **[D]**. *Zero range* is the centre of both circles, the point where the radius has shrunk to nothing **[D]**. In the language of [The Stal King](/posts/the-stal-king/) (DRK-192), it is the stalk: what remains when every neighbourhood around the target has been taken away, and only contact is left **[D]**.

## §2 Words that cut themselves

### §2.1 O range

*Orange* travelled further than most words in English. It begins with Sanskrit *nāraṅga*, passes through Persian *nārang* and Arabic *nāranj*, becomes Italian *narancia* and *arancia*, Old French *orenge*, and arrives in English in the late fourteenth century; as a colour word it appears only in the 1510s, before which a similar colour might be called *citrine* or *saffron* (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. Somewhere along the way it lost its initial *n*, and the dictionary attributes the loss, probably, to the article: *a narange* heard as *an arange* (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**.

That makes the cut *O range*, zero range, more than a joke **[M]**. The word was once cut at that very seam by its own speakers, and the boundary between article and noun moved by one letter **[E]**. Reading the *O* as a zero and the rest as a range is the same operation done a second time, on purpose **[M]**. In the corpus's terms, the language performed a stal-king cut on itself, and we inherited the fruit **[M]**.

### §2.2 Melee: the mixture

*Melee* is French *mêlée*, a confused fight among many, from Old French *meslee*, "brawl, confused fight; mixture, blend", the noun of *mesler* "to mix, mingle"; English borrowed it as *melle* in the Middle Ages, lost it, and borrowed it again in the 1640s (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. Its siblings are *meddle* and *medley* (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**.

The etymology is the best description of zero range ever written **[D]**. At distance, combat is an exchange between two separate objects: a projectile crosses a gap. At zero range the gap closes and the two bodies become one tangle, limbs mixed, breath mixed, blood mixed **[D]**. Military historians who have tried to reconstruct close combat stress the same confusion: the participants themselves could rarely say afterwards what had happened, because there was no outside to see it from (Keegan 1976) **[S]**. A melee is the point at which the observer's position disappears **[D]**.

### §2.3 Salient and assault: the leap

*Salient* came into English in the 1560s as a heraldic term for a leaping beast, from Latin *salire* "to leap"; its sense "pointing outward" is military, from the 1680s, and its modern sense "prominent, striking" descends from *salient point*, the translation of Aristotle's *punctum saliens*, the heart of an embryo that seems to leap (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. *Assault* is the same verb with *ad-*: to leap at **[E]**.

Three corpus threads meet on that Latin verb **[D]**. The cat's final rush in [The Stal King](/posts/the-stal-king/) (DRK-192) is a *saltus*, a leap from the last cover onto the prey **[D]**. The aberrant salience of [The Conviction Gate](/posts/the-conviction-gate/) (DRK-194) is the experience of things leaping out at the mind **[D]**. And the assault is the body leaping at another body, the moment when distance is abolished by force **[D]**. Salience is the leap seen from the side of the one leapt at; assault is the same leap seen from the side of the one leaping **[M]**.

### §2.4 Cramp, claw, clause, Krampus

*Cramp*, the involuntary contraction of a muscle, came into English in the late fourteenth century through Old French *crampe* from a Germanic source; its Old High German relatives mean "cramp, spasm" and "bent, crooked", and the same Germanic *\*kramp-* forms many words for the bent and the hooked, including *crampon* (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. A second *cramp*, a metal bar bent at both ends, appears in the fifteenth century (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. Swedish *kramp* and *krampanfall*, the cramp and the convulsive fit, belong to the same family **[S]**.

*Claw* is a separate Germanic word, Old English *clawu*, "claw, talon, iron hook", and the verb *clawian* "to scratch" (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. *Clause* is Latin: *clausula*, "the end, a closing", from *claudere* "to close, to shut, to conclude" (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. The claw that grips and the clause that closes share nothing but a sound **[M]**. Yet *claudere* also gives *claustrophobia*, and Germanic *\*kramp-* gives *cramped*: two unrelated roots arriving at the same experience of a space that has closed around a body **[E]**.

*Krampus* is attested from 1872 as the figure who assists Saint Nicholas at Christmas by punishing the wicked; older German texts spell him *Grampus* or *Grampes* (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. The etymology is uncertain: one derivation runs from German *Krampen* "claw", another no further than the Germanic base of *cramp* (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[H]**. Either way, the punishing companion of the gift-bringer carries the bent, the hooked and the gripping in his name **[D]**.

And *seize*, which by about 1300 meant "to grip with the hands or teeth", is where [The Earth Engine](/posts/the-earth-engine/) (DRK-193) found two opposite failures, the brain's seizure and the engine's (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. Cramp, claw and seizure are all grips: bent fingers, bent talons, bent muscle **[D]**.

### §2.5 Two bendings

Lay the roots side by side. *Range* comes from *\*sker-*, "to turn, bend"; *cramp* from *\*kramp-*, "bent, crooked" **[E]**. They are different roots that mean the same thing **[E]**. One bending opens outward into a ring, a circle of reach; the other folds inward into a grip, a hook, a muscle locked against itself **[D]**. The whole post lives between those two bendings: the ring that keeps the other at a distance, and the grip that closes on the other at zero range **[D]**.

## §3 Formal sketch: range as a ring (analogy, not derivation)

Let $x$ be a target in a metric space and

$$B(x,r) = \{\, y : d(x,y) < r \,\}$$

the open ball of radius $r$ around it **[E]**. The literal sense of *range* is this ball: a ring of reach **[M]**. In [The Stal King](/posts/the-stal-king/) (DRK-192) the stalk at $x$ was what survives as $r \to 0$ **[D]**.

Let $L(d)$ be the lethality an attacker can deliver at distance $d$, $E(d)$ the attacker's exposure to retaliation at that distance, and $S(d)$ the salience of the target to the attacker, how vividly it is present as a living body **[D]**. For an animal armed with teeth and claws, $L$ is supported near zero, $L(d) \approx 0$ beyond body reach, while $E$ and $S$ are both high at that reach: the prey can bite back, and it is fully present **[D]**.

Ranged weapons change all three functions at once **[D]**. They extend the support of $L$ outward, they make $E(d)$ fall as $d$ grows, and they make $S(d)$ fall too, because a target at three hundred metres is a shape and at three thousand a coordinate **[D]**. The human strategy can be written as the ratio

$$\frac{L(d)}{E(d)} \quad \text{increasing in } d ,$$

harm delivered rising relative to harm risked as distance grows **[D]**. Section §7 adds the third function: with the [Conviction Gate](/posts/the-conviction-gate/) (DRK-194) written as $c \cdot V > \theta$, falling salience lowers the threshold $\theta$ **[D]**.

**Zero range** (proposed corpus term): the limit at which the distance between attacker and target vanishes and the encounter becomes a mixture of bodies rather than an exchange across space **[D]**.

## §4 What animals carry to zero range

### §4.1 The contact killers

The corpus's predators kill in contact **[D]**. Leyhausen's account of cat predation ends, after the long stalk and the motionless lurk, in a short rush, a strike with the forepaws and a bite directed at the neck (Leyhausen 1979) **[S]**. The monitor lizard's predation ends in the bite and the shake, the moment [The Bite](/posts/the-bite/) (DRK-184) treated as the commitment of the whole body to the jaws **[D]**. Wolves, sharks, raptors, crocodiles and mantises all differ in how they close the distance, and they all finish at zero range **[E]**.

The animal body is built for that last centimetre **[D]**. Teeth, talons, claws, horns, spurs, venom fangs and constricting coils are tools for the moment when two bodies touch **[E]**. Armour is the same logic seen from the other side: the turtle's shell, the armadillo's plates and the pangolin's scales are built for a world in which danger arrives in contact, and there is no reason to grow a shell against an arrow **[D]**.

### §4.2 The animals that throw

There are animals that act at a distance, and they are worth looking at closely precisely because they are so few, and so short-ranged **[D]**.

The archerfish lies just under the surface of mangrove water and spits a jet that knocks insects off overhanging leaves. In experiments, archerfish learned to down rapidly moving targets with practice and also by watching other fish do it (Schuster et al. 2006) **[S]**. But the shot does not kill; it brings the prey down to the water, where the fish takes it in its mouth **[S]**. The projectile is a delivery system to zero range, not a substitute for it **[D]**.

The snapping shrimp closes an enlarged claw so fast that a cavitation bubble forms in the water and collapses, producing the sharp snap that gives the animal its name; the effect reaches a few centimetres (Versluis et al. 2000) **[S]**. It is the purest image this post has: the claw itself turned into a short-range projectile weapon, the grip converted into a shock **[D]**.

Velvet worms squirt jets of sticky slime from two head papillae that entangle their prey, and the oscillating jet that does this has been traced to a passive hydrodynamic instability rather than to fine muscular aiming (Concha et al. 2015) **[S]**. The bombardier beetle mixes chemicals in an internal chamber and ejects a hot, pulsed defensive spray (Arndt et al. 2015) **[S]**. Spitting cobras have evolved the ability to project venom at the eyes of threats several times independently, with venom components that cause intense pain (Kazandjian et al. 2021) **[S]**.

Each case has the same shape **[D]**. The range is centimetres, or at most a few metres; the purpose is to capture, stun, blind or deter, rarely to kill outright; and the projectile, where it serves predation at all, hands the prey back to the mouth **[D]**.

### §4.3 Our closest relatives

Chimpanzees throw. At Gombe, Goodall recorded chimpanzees throwing sticks and stones, mostly during aggressive displays and often with poor aim (Goodall 1986) **[S]**. The throw is real and it is social: it intimidates, it impresses, it sometimes hits **[S]**. It is not a way of killing. When chimpanzees kill, prey or rivals, they kill with hands and teeth, at zero range (Goodall 1986) **[S]**.

That is the baseline against which the human exception has to be measured **[D]**. The ape that already throws does not kill by throwing; the ape that kills by throwing is us **[D]**.

## §5 The naked thrower

### §5.1 What we lack

Look at the human body as a zero-range weapon and it fails almost every test **[D]**. Our canines barely project beyond the other teeth; our nails are flat; our skin is thin and nearly hairless; we have no horns, no venom, no shell **[E]**. One of the earliest signs of that disarmament is very old. The canines of *Ardipithecus ramidus*, about 4.4 million years old, show sexual dimorphism almost as low as in modern humans, which the authors read as a reduction of male canine weaponry long before the genus *Homo* (Suwa et al. 2021) **[S]**.

That date is important for this post's thesis, and it is inconvenient **[D]**. If canine reduction came two million years before efficient throwing, then the throw cannot simply have been invented to replace the lost tooth **[D]**. The more careful version of the thesis is that our lineage gave up some zero-range weaponry for social reasons first, lived for a long time with less of it, and then, in *Homo*, found in the throw a way to be dangerous again, at a distance and together **[H]**.

### §5.2 The shoulder as a bow

Roach and colleagues showed that the human shoulder works like a catapult: during the wind-up of a throw, the arm stores elastic energy in ligaments and tendons crossing the shoulder and releases it in an internal rotation faster than any other human movement; from fossil anatomy, they argued that the features enabling this appeared in *Homo* about two million years ago (Roach et al. 2013) **[S]**.

The image is worth slowing down for **[D]**. A body that stores energy in its shoulder and releases it into a projectile is already a bow; the later bow simply moved the spring outside the body **[D]**. The sling, a strap swung to and fro and twisted before release, whose English name comes from Germanic verbs of swinging and winding, is the same idea in leather (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**. Every step of the ladder that follows is an attempt to put more stored energy between the hand and the target **[D]**.

### §5.3 Spear, run and arrow

The oldest well-preserved hunting weapons are the wooden spears of Schöningen in Germany, published by Thieme, who read them as throwing spears used in large-game hunting (Thieme 1997) **[S]**. Their age has been revised to about 300,000 years (Richter & Krbetschek 2015) **[S]**. Whether they were thrown or thrust is still debated **[H]**.

Distance did not only mean projectiles **[D]**. In the persistence-hunting model, humans hunted by tracking an animal through the heat of the day until it collapsed, relying on sweating and on the ability to read its trail (Liebenberg 2006) **[S]**. That is range of another kind: not the distance a stone flies but the distance a human can follow, a ring of pursuit measured in hours rather than metres **[D]**. The kill itself, at the end of the run, was still made close **[S]**.

The bow appears much later. Microresidue and fracture analysis of stone points from Sibudu in South Africa indicate bow-and-arrow use around 64,000 years ago (Lombard & Phillipson 2010) **[S]**. Bone points from Fa-Hien Lena in Sri Lanka show bow-and-arrow hunting of small, fast animals in tropical forest by about 48,000 years ago (Langley et al. 2020) **[S]**. With the bow, the human could kill at a distance where the prey had not yet noticed it, and without coming within reach of horns or teeth **[D]**.

Read as equipment, the kit is a body rebuilt outside the body **[D]**. The spear is an extended claw, the arrow a detached fang, the shield and later the cuirass a carapace carried rather than grown **[D]**. The [Earth Engine](/posts/the-earth-engine/) (DRK-193) called fire an exteriorised tooth; the projectile is an exteriorised claw that no longer needs the arm attached to it **[D]**.

## §6 The ladder of range

What began with a stone became a ladder **[D]**. Each rung pushed the support of $L(d)$ further out and pulled $E(d)$ further down **[D]**.

The sling and the bow made skilled individuals dangerous at tens of metres **[S]**. The story of the shepherd who brings down an armoured giant with a sling-stone has survived three thousand years because it compresses the whole post into one scene: the champion built for zero range, waiting to fight in contact, killed by a boy who never comes within his reach **[M]**.

Keegan's reconstruction of three battles, Agincourt in 1415, Waterloo in 1815 and the Somme in 1916, follows how the experience of combat changed as weapons changed: from the archers and the press of armoured men at Agincourt, through musketry and artillery at Waterloo, to the Somme, where most men were killed by fire from people they never saw (Keegan 1976) **[S]**. Across five centuries, the place of death moved away from the hand **[D]**.

The drone is the current end of the ladder **[D]**. Chamayou argues that it does something no earlier weapon did: it removes the operator from the battlefield entirely, so that one side can kill without being exposed at all, which he reads as the end of the reciprocity of risk that once defined combat (Chamayou 2015) **[S]**. In the notation of §3, $E(d) \to 0$ while $L(d)$ stays high: the ratio becomes unbounded **[D]**. The human strategy has reached its limit, and the limit is a fight in which only one side is present **[D]**.

## §7 Distance and the gate

Grossman argued that the resistance most people feel to killing another person falls with physical and psychological distance, highest in hand-to-hand combat and lowest for the bomber crew and the artillery battery (Grossman 1995) **[H]**. His argument rests partly on contested historical data, and it is tagged here as a hypothesis **[H]**. But its structure fits what the rest of this post has found **[D]**.

At zero range the other is a body, and salient in every sense of §2.3: it leaps at the senses, it breathes, it bleeds, it looks back **[D]**. At long range the other is a mark, a silhouette in a sight, a heat signature on a screen **[D]**. In the gate of [The Conviction Gate](/posts/the-conviction-gate/) (DRK-194), $c \cdot V > \theta$, falling salience lowers the threshold $\theta$: the act needs less conviction because there is less of a person in front of it **[D]**.

That links range to [The Weaponised Conviction](/posts/the-weaponised-conviction/) (DRK-195) **[D]**. Dehumanising rhetoric removes the face from the target with words; distance removes it mechanically **[D]**. The two are complementary, and they are most dangerous together: a target already described as less than human, struck from a distance at which it can no longer be seen as one **[D]**.

## §8 Why melee survives

If the human strategy points outward, why does zero range persist at all **[D]**? Partly because the ladder is never complete: weapons jam, ammunition runs out, and soldiers still train for close combat **[E]**. But mostly because zero range is where something is learned that cannot be learned at a distance **[D]**.

Ritualised combat is the clearest case **[D]**. Many animals settle disputes at zero range without killing, wrestling, pushing and grappling by rules that keep the damage down, as the corpus's varanid clinch did for monitor lizards **[D]**. Human martial arts, wrestling and the training halls of the sword keep the same knowledge alive: what it is to be in contact, to feel another's weight and intention through the hands, and to stop **[D]**. Zero range is where restraint is practised, because only at zero range does the other's resistance arrive as a felt fact **[D]**.

That is the deeper reason the melee matters **[D]**. A culture that only ever acts at range loses the place where the cost of violence is felt in the body **[D]**. The mixture of §2.2 is frightening, but it is also the only setting in which both parties are fully present, and in which the gate is held by both **[D]**.

## §9 The gift and the claw

In the Alpine custom the gift-bringer does not come alone **[E]**. Saint Nicholas is accompanied by Krampus, the horned and chained figure who punishes the wicked children, attested in German usage from the nineteenth century (Online Etymology Dictionary) **[E]**.

Whatever the root of Krampus's name, the pair stages the two ends of §3 **[D]**. The saint acts at a distance: his gifts appear unseen in the night, the giver absent from the act of giving **[D]**. The claw comes to zero range: to the door, to the child, with the switch and the chain **[D]**. Generosity is ranged, punishment is close; the custom has known for centuries what the formal sketch above writes as two functions of distance **[D]**. *Claus* and *claw* share only a sound **[M]**, but the custom sets them side by side anyway, and the custom is older than the pun **[D]**.

## §10 Falsification (DRK-131)

**F1: No lethal animal range.** §4 claims that no non-human animal routinely kills by projectile at tens of metres. **Refuted** by documentation of any species that regularly does.

**F2: Compensation.** §5 claims that efficient throwing restored danger to a lineage that had already reduced its zero-range weaponry. **Refuted** if the fossil and archaeological record places efficient throwing before canine reduction, or shows no link between the two.

**F3: Distance and threshold.** §7 claims that distance lowers the threshold for lethal action. **Refuted** if controlled studies show no relation between distance to target and willingness to harm, with target identity and command pressure held constant.

**Scope.** The word chain in §1–§2 is tagged root by root; the argument survives removal of every [M] link. Range is offered as one axis of human violence, not its explanation.
