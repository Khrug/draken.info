## §0 Sammanfattning

*Munnen har smakat, talat och hållits öppen. Nu stänger den sig, och det är stängningen som gör resten bindande.*

The September run built an orality cluster: tasting in DRK-180 *Jacob's Organ*, speaking in DRK-181 *The Countable Mouth*, forced non-closure in [DRK-182 *The Held Mouth*](/posts/the-held-mouth/). [DRK-183 *The Keeper at the Crossing*](/posts/the-keeper-at-the-crossing/) then moved to the gate. The mouth's fourth function, the bite, was treated only negatively. This post pays that debt.

**The headline claim is a typology** **[D]**. Any guarantee faces the same problem: the stake must be held somewhere, and whoever holds it can abuse it. There are four answers:

| Answer | Where the stake sits | Instance |
|---|---|---|
| Pledge | with the counterparty | Týr's hand in Fenrir's mouth |
| Simultaneity | nowhere; both stakes cross at once | *byteshandel*, barter |
| Third term | with a neutral custodian or guarantor | escrow, *borgen* |
| Mutual hostage | with each party, inside the other's reach | the varanid clinch |

Expropriation, the sword in Fenrir's jaws, is the fifth case and the null answer: no stake, no consent.

Everything else in the post supports this table. The etymologies of §3, §8 and §9 show that Germanic languages name commitment through posture, garment and hostage. **They illustrate the structures; nothing here claims they cause them** (F5). Remorse (§6) and the lexical operator of §10 are the most speculative parts and are tagged accordingly.

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[E]** | Established: the text says it, or the etymology is standard (SAOB, Hellquist, OED) |

## §1 The fourth function

The mouth has four classical functions: taste, speech, breath and bite **[S]**. Taste and speech exchange signal. The bite is the only oral function that commits one body to an irreversible act on another. It is therefore the only one that can carry a guarantee **[D]**.

This returns the cluster to the framework's oldest motif, the clinch of DRK-123, and to the keeper of [DRK-150 *The Generalizard*](/posts/the-generalizard/), whose authority rests on a capacity held rather than spent.

## §2 Withheld, prevented, executed

**Withheld: the varanid clinch.** Male combat in monitor lizards is organised into distinct phases, with bipedal grappling at its centre, and asymmetries in size strongly shape duration and outcome (Earley, Attum & Eason 2002) **[E]**. Biting is rare in the ritual phase and belongs mainly to escalation **[S]**; the same review notes that most varanid combat data are qualitative. The bite exists and is fully capable. It is not spent.

**Prevented: Fenrir and the sword.** After Gleipnir holds, the gods set a sword in the wolf's jaws, hilt against the lower jaw and point against the palate **[E]**. This is the forced non-closure of DRK-182. The bite is made impossible from outside.

**Executed: Týr's hand.** Fenrir accepts the fetter only if one of the gods places a hand in his mouth as a pledge that the binding is made without deceit, and that he will be released if he cannot break it **[E]**. Týr does. The fetter holds, the gods do not release him, and Fenrir bites the hand off at the wrist **[E]**. Snorri adds that the joint is therefore called *úlfliðr*, the wolf-joint; commentators treat the etymology as Snorri's own **[S]**.

**The breach is the gods'** **[E]**. Fenrir did not break the pledge. He executed it exactly as designed, against a counterparty that had committed fraud before the deal was struck. The gods entered knowing they would not release him.

This changes what the myth proves **[D]**. The pledge did not fail mechanically. Fenrir judged correctly and enforced on time. It failed as a *deterrent*, because the defrauding party had already priced the collateral in. **Týr's hand was the premium paid for a rigged deal.** The gods wanted the wolf bound more than they wanted the hand, so they paid.

Two ledgers are in the text, and both matter **[D]**. The pledge ledger clears: the hand is forfeited on time. The binding ledger does not: the fetter was the fraud, and it stays open until Ragnarök. Paying the collateral settled the pledge. It did not settle the thing the pledge was supposed to guarantee.

## §3 The pledge vocabulary

Collateral begins as persons, garments and body parts before it becomes money. The vocabulary keeps the trace.

**Veð, vad, wed, gage.** Snorri's word for the pledge is Old Norse *veð* **[E]**. Its cognates are Swedish *vad* (wager), English *wed* (a pledge; a wedding is a pledge-making) and Gothic *wadi* **[E]**. The same Germanic word, borrowed through Frankish into Old French, returns as *gage*, and from there English *wage* and *engage* **[E]**. Medieval Latin *wadium*, the pledge in legal records, is the same word again **[E]**. Latin *vas, vadis* (surety) is commonly placed in the set **[S]**.

**Mortgage** is Old French *mort gage*, a "dead pledge" **[E]**. It belongs to the death family of *mors*, not the bite family of *mordere*; §6 returns to this.

**Pant.** Swedish *pant* comes from Middle Low German *pant*, and German *Pfand* is the same word **[E]**. Its deeper origin is uncertain. One proposal runs through Old French *pan*, a piece of cloth that also meant a pledge, from Latin *pannus*, cloth **[H]**. If it holds, the pledge was once the cloak taken off and handed over. The garment reading is suggestive, not established.

**Hostage and gisslan.** English *hostage* comes from Old French *(h)ostage*, the state of a guest lodged with a host **[S]**. Latin *hospes* (guest, host) contains *hostis* (stranger, later enemy) **[S]**. Swedish *gisslan* goes back to Old Norse *gísl*, cognate with German *Geisel* **[E]**. The hostage was a guest held as security: **hospitality with a human stake** **[D]**.

**Insats.** From *sätta in*, to set in **[E]**. The stake is *that which is placed inside*. Týr's hand is the most literal *insats* in the Germanic record.

**Collateral.** Latin *col-* + *latus*, side **[E]**. Collateral security runs *alongside* the principal obligation. The hand is not the deal; the fettering is.

**Säkerhet.** From *säker*, via Middle Low German *seker*, from Latin *securus*: *se-* (apart from) + *cura* (care) **[E]**. Security is the state in which the holder may stop caring. Swedish holds safety and collateral in one word, *ställa säkerhet*.

**Borgen.** A *borgensman* is a third party who answers for another's debt with his own assets **[E]**. *Borgen* is therefore not the Týr case. It is a third-term answer: the guarantee comes from outside the dyad.

**Escrow.** Anglo-French *escrowe*, a scroll or strip of parchment **[E]**; the link to *shred* is often proposed and disputed **[H]**. The scroll was the deed held back until a condition was met. The separation of holding from enforcing is an institutional feature of escrow, not something the etymology delivers **[D]**.

## §4 Four answers to a held stake

A pledge puts the stake with the counterparty. The counterparty is then holder, judge and enforcer in one organ. The other three answers each remove one of those roles from the counterparty's jaw.

**Simultaneity: byteshandel.** In barter both stakes cross the overlap at once. Nobody holds anything for anybody, so no custodian is needed **[D]**. The price is the double coincidence of wants: each side must want what the other has, at the same time (Jevons 1875) **[E]**. Simultaneity removes the holder by removing time.

**Third term: escrow and borgen.** A neutral custodian holds the stake (escrow), or a third party answers for it (*borgen*) **[E]**. The counterparty keeps its claim but loses custody. This removes the holder by adding a party.

**Mutual hostage: the clinch.** In ritual combat, neither animal surrenders anything and no third party holds anything. Each body lies inside the other's bite range, and each withholds **[D]**. **The clinch's *insats* is exposure**: the committed risk that escalation will cost the escalator as much as the target. Williamson (1983) showed that exchanged hostages can support exchange where no court enforces it; Schelling (1960) made the same point for mutual deterrence **[E]**. The clinch is that mechanism in a body.

This corrects an earlier draft of this post, which filed the clinch under escrow. That was wrong. Escrow needs a deposit and a holder, and the clinch has neither **[D]**.

| Answer | Holder | Judge of breach | Cost | Instance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pledge | counterparty | counterparty | deterrence fails if collateral < gain from breach | Týr |
| Simultaneity | none | none needed | double coincidence of wants | *byteshandel* |
| Third term | custodian or guarantor | the stated condition | the third party must be trusted | escrow, *borgen* |
| Mutual hostage | each party | each party | works only between near-equals | the clinch |
| Expropriation | the coercer | the coercer | no consent; debt accrues | the sword |

The last column of the mutual-hostage row matters. Earley and colleagues report that size asymmetry drives contest outcome **[E]**. The clinch is available only when each body is a credible threat to the other. Under strong asymmetry it collapses into pledge or sword **[D]**.

## §5 Who measures the breach

§4 asks who *holds* the stake. The deeper question is who *detects* the breach **[D]**.

In the pledge, detection belongs to the counterparty's own jaw. In the myth the jaw measured correctly. In general it has an incentive not to, because declaring breach pays the declarer. A holder who profits from detecting breach will tend to find it.

Escrow's real function is therefore **exogeneity of the measurement channel**: the agent who decides that breach occurred must not profit from the decision **[D]**. This is the endogenous-measurement problem of DRK-168 and DRK-169 in contractual form.

The clinch solves it differently. It does not make measurement exogenous; it makes it **symmetric**. Each animal measures the other's escalation, and a false declaration (biting first) triggers the escalation it claims to answer **[D]**. Declaring breach costs the declarer.

This partly answers DRK-183's open recursion, "who keeps the keeper" **[D]**. Among near-equals, no keeper is needed: mutual exposure keeps both parties. Among unequals, the recursion stays open, because the clinch is unavailable (§4). That remainder belongs to the security post still to come.

## §6 Re-mordere: the second bite

Latin *mordere*, to bite, maps the life cycle of a bite **[E]**:

- *morsel*, via Old French, diminutive of *mors*, a bite, from *morsus*: the bite taken, and the unit of the bite.
- *mordant*: the bite as a persistent property, a substance that keeps biting.
- *remorse*, via Late Latin *remorsus*, from *remordere*, to bite again: the bite that returns.

**Reading** **[D]**. Remorse is the bite not executed at the moment of breach, and therefore executed later, inward. It is opened by a breach one commits without paying the forfeiture. It is closed by paying late: reparation.

The counterparty carries the other half. *Once bitten, twice shy*; *bränt barn skyr elden* **[E]** for the proverbs. The prior bite leaves a record that changes all later exchange: the bitten party demands more collateral, or refuses to post any. Trust, on this reading, is the willingness to post a stake, and wariness is the memory of a bite **[D]**.

This gives the reading one operational handle, stated as a hypothesis in F4: **demand for collateral should rise after breach and fall after reparation**. The framework's coherence-debt notation is deliberately not used here. Whether remorse is a formal case of $K(t)$ is left open until it has more content than this handle.

**The trap: *morsus* vs *mors*.** *Mors*, death, belongs with *mori*, to die, from PIE \*mer- **[E]**. *Mordere* is usually connected to a different reconstruction, often given as \*(s)merd- **[H]**. The two families only sound alike **[M]**. *Mortgage* (§3) shows the trap from the other side: it looks like a bite-word and is a death-word, the "dead pledge" **[E]**.

### §6.1 Smärta och смерть: the trap, twice

The *morsus/mors* trap is not a Latin accident. It recurs independently in Germanic and Slavic.

| Bite / harm family (\*mer-, "rub away, harm") | Death family (\*mer-, "die") |
|---|---|
| Latin *mordere*, *morsus*, *remorse* | Latin *mors*, *mortgage* |
| Swedish *smärta*, English *smart*, German *Schmerz* **[S]** | Russian *смерть* pronounced *smert*, Swedish *mord*, English *murder* **[E]** |

*Smärta* is the bite that stays: German *schmerzen* first meant "to bite" **[E]**, and the Germanic set is probably cognate with *mordere* **[S]**. *Смерть* is Proto-Slavic \*sъ- + \*mьrtь, from the root of *mors* **[E]**. The initial s- in each is a different morpheme **[M]**. Three pairs, three near-collisions: *morsus/mors*, *smärta/смерть*, *mordere/mord*.

The languages keep apart what the sound runs together **[D]**. A bite, however painful, is a restriction the body survives. Pain is the section that remains after a bite; death is the section that no longer exists. Whether the two \*mer- roots were ever one is open **[H]** (cf. Greek *marainein*, "waste away"). The post does not lean on it.

The pledge vocabulary knew where pain lands. Proverbs 11:15 in the King James Version: *He that is surety for a stranger shall smart for it.* The *borgensman* of §3 is the one who feels the bite.

## §7 Bit, byte, bite, bridle

DRK-181 made the mouth countable. The lexical record gives it a unit.

| Word | Relation to *bite* | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| *bit* (small piece) | Old English *bita*, a piece bitten off, from *bītan* | **[E]** derivation |
| *bit* (bridle mouthpiece) | Old English *bite*, act of biting: the part the horse bites | **[E]** derivation |
| *bit* (unit of information) | clipping of *binary digit*, credited to John Tukey in Shannon (1948) | **[E]** clipping |
| *byte* | coined by Werner Buchholz at IBM in 1956 during the Stretch project | **[E]** coinage |
| *byte* as respelled *bite* | Buchholz's own later accounts (1977, 1981); IBM's project history adds that staff already used *bite* for small character-sized segments | **[S]** rationale |

The technical *bit* is a clipping that lands on a word already derived from *bite*. On Buchholz's own account, *byte* respells *bite* to avoid confusion with *bit*. The documentation of the rationale is retrospective, so it is tagged **[S]**. The byte was originally one to six bits and moved to eight later in 1956 **[E]**.

The mapping must stay a metaphor **[D]**. A bit is one distinction, not one closure of a jaw. The jaw offers an image of a binary event, open or closed. It is not a claim about the engineering.

**The bridle bit** is the stronger find **[D]**. It is the sword's civil successor. The sword prevents the mouth from closing. The bit gives the mouth something to close on, and routes the closure through reins. Governance through the mouth, not against it. The idioms complete the failure modes:

- *champing at the bit*: the governed straining against the instrument;
- *taking the bit between the teeth*: the governed seizes the instrument so it no longer transmits the rider's signal. The control device is captured by the party it controls.

## §8 Byte: prey, exchange, changeling

*Käften tar; bytet delar ut.* The jaw takes; the *byte* distributes.

Swedish *byte* is not an English *byte*, and not a bite. The two collide in spelling only **[M]**.

**Byta**: Old Swedish *bȳta*, from Middle Low German *būten* **[E]**. Its oldest Swedish sense is *dela, skifta, fördela*: to divide shares **[E]** (SAOB, sense 1). A nautical sense is *portionera ut mat*, to deal out rations **[E]**. Hellquist derives it probably from \*bi-ûtian, "give out" **[S]**, and explicitly rejects a root meaning "to cut off" **[E]** for his position.

**Byte** (booty): Old Swedish *bȳte*, from Middle Low German *būte*, which also gave German *Beute* and French *butin* **[E]**. *Fribytare*, freebooter, is one who takes *byte* freely **[E]**.

So Swedish *byte* is **the sharing-out of what was taken**. Its two senses mark the axis of §2 and §4 **[D]**. *Rovdjurets byte* is the unilateral taking, the bite without agreement. *Byteshandel* is simultaneous exchange, the answer that needs no holder. *Bytt är bytt och kommer aldrig igen* **[E]** for the proverb: once exchanged, closed like a jaw.

**Bortbyting.** Hellquist records *byting* as a troll child left *i utbyte* for a stolen human child **[E]**. It is exchange with one party absent. In DRK-183's terms the changeling is a **counterfeit section**: it passes the local check (a child in the cradle) and fails to glue with the family's section over time **[D]**. Byteshandel is exchange across the overlap with both present. Bortbyting is substitution with one absent, like Gleipnir sold to Fenrir as a test.

## §9 Lagt kort ligger: the posture spine

Swedish builds its commitment vocabulary from posture verbs **[D]**. The table is the spine of this section.

| Verb | Commitment words | What is committed |
|---|---|---|
| *sätta* (set) | *insats* | the stake (§3) |
| *lägga* (lay) | *lag* (law), *lag* (team), *félag*, *lagt kort* | the rule, the company, the play |
| *ligga* (lie) | *samlag*, *tidelag* | the bodily union |
| *latus* (side, Latin) | *collateral* | security beside the deal |

**Lägga → lag.** Old Swedish *lagh*, law, is related to *lägga* **[E]**: the law is what is laid down. English *law* is borrowed from Old Norse *lagu*, plural of *lag* **[E]**. *Lag* as team is the same word, those laid together **[E]**: *arbetslag*, *fotbollslag*.

The link to §3 is exact **[E]**. Old Norse *félag*, partnership, is *fé* (property) + *lag* (laying): property laid together. A *félagi* is one who has laid down a share, and English *fellow* comes from it. **Fellowship is joint collateral.** A *lag* in the old sense is a set of parties who have each posted an *insats*: the mutual hostage of §4, made civil.

**Byalag** joins two roots that only sound related to *byte* **[M]**. *By* (village) belongs with *bo, bygga*, Old Norse *búa*, to dwell **[E]**. The byalag is the dwelling community that lays down rules for shared land **[S]**: the commons of Ostrom (1990), and the keeper-function of DRK-183 distributed across neighbours.

**Ligga → samlag.** *Sam-* + *lag*, lying together **[E]**. The sexual sense is attested from the early 1600s; Swedberg in the 1720s still glosses it as *sällskap*, and Strindberg in the 1880s uses it for a gathering **[S]**. Company and coupling are one word here, as team and law are.

**Lagt kort ligger** **[E]** for the card-table rule. What is laid lies; it cannot be picked back up. With *bytt är bytt* and the closed jaw, it is the same irreversibility in three registers **[D]**.

## §10 The Tidelag Operator

*Tidelag* shows law acting on language.

**The record** **[E]**. Old Swedish *þȳþilagh* is formed from *þȳdhas*, to have intercourse with (the verb behind *ty sig till*, to attach oneself to), and *lag* in the sense of lying together. The word first meant any intercourse. The recurring medieval legal formula *hava þȳþilagh mæþ fǣ* narrowed it to intercourse with animals. The form was later reshaped after *tīdhas*, to mate (Hellquist; SAOB).

**The operator** **[D]**. The *lag* that is written (law) acted as a restriction map on the *lag* that is lived (lying), until the legal context was the only one left. Stated generally:

> **A restriction applied often enough becomes the definition.**

English shows the same narrowing by other routes **[E]**. *Intercourse* first meant dealings and commerce. *Copulation* first meant any coupling or linking. *Ejaculate* first meant to throw out or exclaim. Those narrowed through euphemism, not legal formula, so *tidelag* remains the clean legal case.

**The cost** **[D]**. Narrowing is lexical amputation. The lost senses are erased variety, and the word can no longer glue onto its own history: *tidelag* can never again mean union in general. It is DRK-158's burnt section at the scale of a single word.

## §11 Formal sketch (analogy, not derivation)

The earlier draft wrote the pledge as a symmetric gluing condition. That was wrong for Týr: only Týr posts a stake. The two cases need different geometry **[D]**.

**Týr: a one-sided restriction.** Let $U_T$ be Týr's body and $R_F$ the reach of Fenrir's jaw. The collateral lives on $W = U_T \cap R_F$, a region of Týr alone. Let the outcome space split into strata, $\Omega = \Omega_{h} \sqcup \Omega_{b}$ (honoured, breached). The pledge is a section over $W \times \Omega$:

$$c\big|_{W \times \Omega_h} = \text{returned}, \qquad c\big|_{W \times \Omega_b} = \text{forfeited}: \; s_T \mapsto s_T\big|_{U_T \setminus W}$$

Forfeiture is the cut along the breach stratum. The wolf-joint is $\partial W$, where the restriction cuts the body.

**The clinch: a genuine overlap.** Let $U_A, U_B$ be two bodies with reaches $R_A, R_B$. The mutual exposure region is

$$O = (U_A \cap R_B) \cup (U_B \cap R_A)$$

Here both parties hold stakes, and the ritual holds exactly when both local sections agree to withhold:

$$\text{ritual holds} \iff s_A\big|_{O} = s_B\big|_{O}$$

**This** is a gluing condition, over a shared region with both parties' stakes in it. The claim "collateral lives on the overlap" is true of the clinch and false of Týr. **That is the reason Týr's arrangement fails and the clinch holds.**

## §12 Governance consequences

Four consequences follow for any system of agents, human or artificial **[D]**:

1. **Collateral must be exchanged, not merely posted.** A stake held inside one party's reach secures the other party only. The *félag* works because every *fé* is laid in common.
2. **Collateral must exceed the gain from breach.** Otherwise it is a premium, not a deterrent, as the gods demonstrated.
3. **The judge of breach must not profit from finding it.** Either make measurement exogenous (escrow, *borgen*) or make it symmetric and costly to the declarer (the clinch).
4. **Prevention is not security.** The sword gives no *säkerhet*: no *insats* and no consent. It freezes the mouth and lets debt accrue until release (DRK-176).

For AI governance the mapping is direct. A hard stop is a sword. A bridle is steering through the mouth, and can be captured. A staked commitment is a pledge. A capability held under mutual exposure is the clinch, and it is available only between near-equals.

The framework's preference follows: mutual hostage where parties are equal, a third term where they are not, pledge only when the collateral exceeds the gain, and the sword never as a substitute for any of them.

## §13 Falsification (DRK-131)

**F1: Ritual bite suppression.** §2 requires biting to be rare in the ritual phase and concentrated in escalation. **Refuted** if quantitative data (Frýdlová material and later work) show comparable bite rates in ritual and escalated phases.

**F2: Symmetry condition.** §4 predicts that ritualised, bite-free contests are more common between size-matched opponents and give way to escalation or fast retreat under strong asymmetry. **Refuted** if bite-free ritual combat is as common between strongly mismatched opponents.

**F3: Third-term advantage.** §5 predicts fewer disputed forfeitures where the stake-holder does not judge breach. **Refuted** if comparative dispute data show no difference or the reverse.

**F4: Collateral after breach.** §6 predicts that demand for collateral rises after a breach and falls after reparation. **Refuted** if trust-game or contract data show no such movement.

**F5: Scope limit.** Nothing here claims that the etymologies cause the governance structures. If F1–F4 fail, §3 and §8–§10 reduce to observations about how Germanic languages name commitment. Every **[E]** stands or falls with SAOB, Hellquist or OED; the **[M]** links carry no weight.
